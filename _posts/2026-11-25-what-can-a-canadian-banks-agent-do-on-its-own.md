Suppose Nora Ellis applies online for a chequing account. Her utility bill confirms the address on the application. A birth certificate gives the date of birth she supplied, under the name Nora Bell. A change of last name would explain the discrepancy. So would a document that belongs to someone else. An AI agent could read every field correctly and still leave the bank with a decision to make.

One of the key opportunities in onboarding lies in deciding which reviews the bank can retire, and which ones it must keep. Where management can specify in advance the evidence sufficient to progress an application, requiring an employee to reach the same conclusion on every file adds cost and delay. Where the evidence supports more than one interpretation, the bank needs a person with the authority to resolve the question. A sound delegation policy gives the agent considerable latitude to prepare a case, gives routine applicants a faster route to an account, and reserves experienced bankers’ time for decisions that warrant it.

## What TD delegated

TD Bank’s Canadian mortgage business offers a useful precedent. In mortgage pre-adjudication, agents classify documents, extract application details, calculate income, and check the figures against selected lending requirements. They also verify consent and flag discrepancies. The underwriter receives a memo that brings the relevant findings together (TD, 2026a). In October 2026, TD reported that average processing time for this stage had fallen from 15 hours to under three minutes. Employees review the outputs and remain responsible for decisions requiring judgment (TD, 2026b).

The most instructive feature of TD’s design is the underwriter’s memo. TD defined a piece of work with a recognizable beginning and end, and a recipient who could use the result. The reported time reduction applies to that preparatory stage (TD, 2026a, 2026b). Considering a similar investment in deposits requires a different figure: the staff time a bank can actually release once a verified application is ready to proceed. An hour taken out of a customer’s wait and an hour returned to a banker are both valuable. They have different implications for the budget.

## The decision inside an account application

Consider how that distinction plays out in Nora’s application. With the necessary permissions, the agent collects the submitted records, extracts the relevant fields, and associates each finding with its source. For this application, the bank has chosen one of the identity verification methods recognized by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Under this *dual-process* method, the bank can confirm name and address from one reliable source and name and date of birth from a different reliable source. The information must be valid and current, and the bank must retain the prescribed verification records. FINTRAC lists utility bills and birth certificates among the possible sources for those respective checks (FINTRAC, 2024).

Had both records borne the name on Nora’s application, the remaining decision would have been relatively straightforward. The agent would run the bank’s existing checks for duplicate customer records, fraud, and applicable screening, recording the outcome of each. Once all required evidence was present and no unresolved finding blocked progress, a rules service operating independently of the agent could authorize the application to advance. Management would already have made the substantive decision when it approved those conditions. An employee repeating the document comparison would contribute little to this particular file.

Advancing the application still leaves a consequential choice to Nora. Permission to examine her documents is separate from her express consent to open an account. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) sets out the bank’s obligations to obtain that consent, enter into an agreement, and provide the customer with a copy (FCAC, 2026). In the proposed design, the core banking system creates the account once the requirements are satisfied. A unique application reference prevents a second account from being opened if a delayed response causes the request to be sent twice. The bank can then trace the new relationship back to the evidence on which it was opened and carry it into ongoing monitoring (FINTRAC, 2025).

Nora’s actual file takes the more revealing route. The agent leaves identity verification incomplete and sends a reviewer the two surnames, the relevant excerpt from each source, and the checks already completed. A useful referral narrows the question enough that the reviewer can act immediately. Is another reliable source available to establish Nora’s identity, or would a different permitted verification method serve her better? FINTRAC allows for minor variations in identifying information and specifies alternatives when a source cannot be relied upon (FINTRAC, 2024). The bank’s proposed policy reserves the interpretation of a material discrepancy for an authorized reviewer. A plausible account of how Nora acquired a different surname carries no evidentiary weight by itself.

## Who owns the unresolved file?

For Nora, the quality of the bank’s service now depends on what happens after the referral. A general request to resubmit identification leaves her to guess which document caused the problem. A reviewer who can see the conflicting fields can ask for what is missing, explain the next step, and preserve the work Nora has already done. Where the digital route proves unsuitable, staff should be able to arrange assistance through another channel. FCAC’s guidance on accommodating non-standard identification addresses requests made in person (FCAC, 2023). A pending digital application should remain distinguishable from a formal refusal, for which the bank must provide written notice (FCAC, 2026).

That distinction has a staffing consequence. Once routine applications move automatically, a larger proportion of the work reaching employees will involve ambiguity. Forecasting reviewer capacity from the historical average time per application will understate the demands of the remaining work. Case ownership matters, too. An identity specialist can resolve conflicting records. A possible screening match belongs with the team authorized to assess it. A verification service that fails to respond calls for a retry and, if the delay persists, an update to the customer. Sending all three to a common queue spends scarce judgment on technical interruptions while customers who need a decision wait.

The manager responsible for account opening should own the result across both routes. That means reviewing completion and waiting times for routine applicants and for customers referred to staff. Risk and compliance should agree with operations on the conditions that warrant suspending automatic progression. Suppose a data provider changes its response and incomplete records begin passing a check. Operations needs the authority to stop further applications from advancing and the records to establish which customers were affected. The ability to reconstruct those decisions is part of the business case for delegating them.

Nora’s application illustrates a distinction that extends beyond account opening. A bank can set the conditions under which a routine application advances and leave a banker to resolve conflicting evidence. The same division of work could apply to card disputes: an agent can assemble transaction records and route the case, while a specialist decides a contested claim. The opportunity for bank leaders is to make routine decisions once, through policy, rather than ask employees to make them again on every file. A bank can then handle more volume without adding manual reviews at the same pace or making customers like Nora wait longer for a decision.
